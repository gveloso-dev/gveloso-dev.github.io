How to find your IBS food triggers, without guessing

Most people with IBS suspect food, and most guess wrong at least once: they cut out something that was innocent and keep eating the thing that wasn’t. Here’s a calmer, more reliable way to find out.

First, rule out the things that aren’t IBS

See a doctor before you start, and again if anything changes. Some symptoms need checking rather than tracking: blood in your stool, losing weight without trying, symptoms that wake you at night, a fever, new symptoms after age 50, or a family history of bowel cancer, celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease. A celiac blood test only works while you’re still eating gluten, so get it done before you change your diet.

1. Log meals and symptoms, with times

The timing is the whole point. Symptoms from FODMAPs usually start a few hours after a meal, once the food reaches the large intestine, so “what did I eat this morning?” matters more than “what did I eat just now?”. Log:

  • Every meal and snack, including drinks, sauces and dressings. Garlic and onion hide in most of them.
  • Each symptom when it starts, with how bad it is: bloating, pain, gas, urgency, heartburn.
  • Bowel movements on the Bristol stool scale (type 1 is hard lumps, type 7 is watery).
  • Sleep and stress, roughly. Both change how your gut reacts, and leaving them out makes food look guiltier than it is.

2. Give it two weeks before you judge

One bad evening after pizza proves nothing. You need several meals with a food and several without it to see a difference. Two weeks of normal eating is usually enough for the foods you eat often to stand out. Rarely eaten foods take longer.

3. Compare, don’t just count

The question isn’t “did I get bloated after garlic?” but “did I get bloated more often after garlic than after everything else?”. If you’re bloated after half of all your meals, a food that’s followed by bloating half the time tells you nothing.

Watch for passengers, too. If you always have garlic bread with pasta, the bread’s wheat and the garlic will look equally guilty. Look at the meals where one appears without the other.

4. Test what you suspect, on purpose

Patterns in a diary are a lead, not proof. The way to confirm one is the low-FODMAP approach, which guidelines from the American College of Gastroenterology and the British Society of Gastroenterology both recommend for IBS, ideally with a dietitian:

  • Swap out high-FODMAP foods for 2 to 6 weeks, until symptoms settle.
  • Reintroduce one FODMAP group at a time (fructans, lactose, fructose, polyols, GOS) over three days, in rising doses, and note what happens.
  • Bring back everything you tolerate. The strict phase isn’t meant to last; a varied diet is better for your gut.

Most people react to one or two groups, not all of them.

5. Bring it to your appointment

A few weeks of dated meals, symptoms and stool types gives your doctor or dietitian far more to go on than memory. Bring the questions you want answered, too.
