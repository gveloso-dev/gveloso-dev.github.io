The Bristol stool chart, explained

Doctors use the Bristol scale because “normal”, “loose” and “hard” mean different things to different people. It sorts stools into seven types, and the type mostly reflects how long stool spent in your gut.

The seven types

  • Type 1: separate hard lumps, hard to pass. Slow transit; constipation.
  • Type 2: sausage-shaped but lumpy. Also on the constipated side.
  • Type 3: sausage-shaped with cracks on the surface. Normal.
  • Type 4: smooth and soft, like a sausage or snake. Normal, and the type most people aim for.
  • Type 5: soft blobs with clear edges, easy to pass. Toward the loose side.
  • Type 6: fluffy, mushy pieces with ragged edges. Loose.
  • Type 7: watery, no solid pieces. Diarrhea.

Types 3 and 4 are the usual target. Types 1 and 2 point toward constipation, 6 and 7 toward diarrhea. With IBS, many people swing between both ends.

How often is normal?

Anywhere from three times a day to three times a week is common. What matters more is a change from what’s usual for you, and whether it’s comfortable.

Why tracking the type helps

IBS is grouped by stool type: IBS-C (mostly types 1–2), IBS-D (mostly 6–7) and IBS-M (both). The type guides treatment, so a few weeks of logged types is one of the most useful things to bring to an appointment. Tracked alongside meals, it can also show whether certain foods line up with looser or harder days.

When to see a doctor

See a doctor promptly for blood in or on your stool, black or tarry stools, diarrhea that wakes you at night, losing weight without trying, or a lasting change in your bowel habit, especially after age 50. Severe pain, a high fever or signs of dehydration need care the same day.
